You are black; you move first. The fly answers. Candidates are empty squares within two cells of a stone. Immediate five-threats are taken as game rules; the rest is the mushroom body. Hover a square (long-press on a phone) for its four class drives.

Kenyon cells idle

Bar length is how much less drive that class receives. Lowest wins. Twenty-four ticks per class are the individual MBONs.

What this is

The mushroom body is not a policy network. It does not choose a square. For every empty point it is shown a 9×9 window and reports a valence — WIN, BLOCK, GOOD, or NEUTRAL — the way a fly maps an odour onto approach or avoidance. We take the best-smelling square.

It has no search and no working memory. It is purely reactive. It knows what a live three looks like but cannot read three moves ahead. It will beat a beginner and lose to anyone playing seriously.

Only the mushroom body is simulated, not the whole fly brain. Training imitated a rule-based gomoku teacher, not human games. The dopamine-gated learning rule on Kenyon-cell to output synapses is ours; it is not in the published Shiu et al. model. On an error, synapses onto the correct class are depressed (Hige et al. 2015) and synapses onto the class the fly wrongly picked are potentiated (Handler et al. 2019).

The heatmap is the point of the page. When you leave an open three, watch which squares ignite.
